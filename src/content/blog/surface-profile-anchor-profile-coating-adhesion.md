A surface can look perfectly clean — no rust, no mill scale, no visible contamination — and still fail a coating spec. Cleanliness and profile are two different requirements, and coating adhesion depends on both. Profile is the one that gets missed most often, because unlike rust, you can't always see it well enough to judge it by eye.

## What Anchor Profile Actually Is

Anchor profile is the microscopic texture of peaks and valleys that abrasive blasting cuts into a metal surface. Industrial coatings don't just sit on top of steel — they mechanically key into that texture, and the depth and consistency of the profile determines how well the coating grips the substrate. Too shallow a profile and the coating has nothing to bite into; adhesion fails early, often showing up as peeling or disbonding within the first year or two of service. Too deep or too sharp a profile, and peaks can poke through thin coating films, creating early rust-through points called "profile peaks" or "holidays" at high spots.

Profile is measured in mils (thousandths of an inch), and most industrial coating systems specify a target range — commonly 1.5 to 4.0 mils depending on the coating type and film thickness being applied.

## How Profile Is Measured

**Replica tape (ASTM D4417 Method C).** A compressible foam tape is pressed onto the blasted surface, taking a physical impression of the peaks and valleys, then measured with a micrometer. This is the most common field method because it's fast, low-cost, and produces a documented, retainable record for the project file.

**Depth micrometer / Tooke gauge.** A more precise method that measures the coating or profile depth directly by cutting a small incision and reading the depth optically under magnification. More common in quality-control-intensive projects like tank linings and immersion coatings, where profile documentation is part of a formal inspection package.

**Surface comparators.** Visual/tactile comparator discs give a fast, low-precision estimate of profile range in the field — useful for a quick check during blasting, not for final documentation on a specified project.

## Why This Gets Missed

Profile depth is driven by media type, media size, air pressure, nozzle distance, and blast angle — all variables an experienced operator controls, but all variables that produce inconsistent results without attention. A crew blasting to visual cleanliness standards (SSPC-SP 6, SP 10, etc.) without also checking profile can hit the cleanliness spec and still miss the profile spec entirely, especially with finer abrasive media or media that's been recycled past its effective cutting life.

This is also where media selection interacts with the requirement directly: coarser garnet or steel grit cuts deeper profile than fine glass bead or crushed glass, for the same air pressure and dwell time. Choosing media without accounting for the coating manufacturer's profile requirement is a common cause of profile-related coating failure — see our breakdown of [why industrial coatings fail on surface preparation](/blog/industrial-coating-failure-surface-prep) for how this plays out on real projects.

## What Should Be Documented

For any project where coating warranty or long-term performance matters — structural steel, tanks, bridges, marine and wastewater assets — profile should be measured and recorded, not assumed. A defensible project record includes:

- Media type, size, and blast pressure used
- Profile measurements at multiple representative locations, not just one spot
- Comparison against the coating manufacturer's specified profile range
- Photographic documentation alongside the numeric readings

This record matters twice: it protects the facility if a coating fails prematurely and the cause needs to be identified, and it protects the contractor by proving the surface prep met spec before the first coat went on. See our related post on [tank and vessel surface preparation and SSPC standards](/blog/tank-vessel-surface-preparation-sspc) for how profile fits into a full surface prep specification.

## The Bottom Line

A clean-looking surface isn't the same as a coating-ready surface. Profile is a measurable, specifiable, verifiable requirement — and skipping the measurement step is one of the most common, most avoidable causes of coating adhesion failure on industrial steel.
