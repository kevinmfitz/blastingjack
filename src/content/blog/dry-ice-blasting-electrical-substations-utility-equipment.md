Cleaning electrical equipment has always come with a tradeoff facility and utility managers don't love: the methods aggressive enough to remove carbon tracking, dust buildup, and contamination are usually the same methods that pose a risk around energized components. Water conducts. Abrasive media can damage insulation and leave conductive residue in switchgear cabinets. Dry ice blasting removes that tradeoff — it's non-conductive, leaves no moisture, and produces no residue that could compromise an electrical enclosure.

## Why Dry Ice Blasting Is Suited to Electrical Equipment

CO₂ pellets are a poor conductor and sublimate on contact, meaning there's no water, no abrasive grit, and no chemical film left inside an enclosure after cleaning. That combination is why dry ice blasting has become a standard maintenance tool for substations, switchgear rooms, and utility equipment where downtime for a full outage is expensive and, in some cases, not something a utility can schedule on short notice.

This doesn't mean every job is done on live equipment — that determination is always made by the facility's electrical safety protocols and qualified personnel, not the cleaning contractor. But the fact that dry ice blasting doesn't introduce moisture or debris into an enclosure is what makes it viable for a much wider range of scheduling scenarios than water or abrasive cleaning would allow.

## Where It's Used on Electrical and Utility Equipment

**Transformers.** Dust, oil residue, and surface contamination on transformer housings and cooling fins can be cleaned without introducing moisture near oil-filled components or electrical bushings.

**Switchgear and breaker cabinets.** Carbon tracking, dust accumulation, and contamination inside switchgear enclosures are cleaned without leaving conductive residue behind — a real concern with abrasive media, which can lodge in gaps and connection points.

**Substation structural equipment.** Steel structures, bus bar supports, and enclosure exteriors at substations accumulate dirt, bird droppings, and atmospheric grime that dry ice blasting removes without the water runoff a pressure washer would generate on a site with sensitive electrical grounding considerations.

**Generator and motor control centers.** Industrial motor control equipment and backup generator housings benefit from a cleaning method that doesn't require full disassembly or introduce moisture into control panels.

**Insulator cleaning.** Contamination buildup on insulators, which can contribute to flashover risk in certain conditions, can be addressed with dry ice blasting without abrasive damage to the insulator surface.

## Coordinating the Work

Any work around energized electrical equipment requires coordination with the facility's electrical safety program — arc flash boundaries, PPE requirements, and lockout/tagout procedures where applicable are determined by the facility, not assumed by the cleaning crew. Dry ice blasting crews working in these environments should be briefed on the facility's specific electrical safety requirements before work begins, the same as any other contractor working near energized equipment.

Ventilation is also a factor inside enclosed switchgear rooms or below-grade vaults — CO₂ sublimation requires adequate air exchange, which needs to be planned for confined electrical spaces the same as any other enclosed work area.

## What It Doesn't Address

Dry ice blasting removes surface contamination — it doesn't repair insulation breakdown, replace failing components, or substitute for infrared inspection and preventive maintenance testing. It's a cleaning method that supports a broader electrical maintenance program, not a replacement for the electrical testing and inspection that identifies failing equipment in the first place.
